Inherited Cardiomyopathy: Cardiac Imaging and Tissue Characterization
This repository examines how cardiac imaging helps characterize inherited cardiomyopathies, with a focus on cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), myocardial tissue characterization, and the interpretation of imaging findings across inherited cardiomyopathy phenotypes.

The central idea is that inherited cardiomyopathy is not defined by ventricular size or pumping function alone. Changes in myocardial tissue, including fibrosis, scar, fatty replacement, inflammation, and altered tissue composition, can contribute important information about the disease phenotype.
Scope
The repository covers:
- The structural and tissue-level features of inherited cardiomyopathies
- The role of cardiac MRI in identifying and characterizing myocardial abnormalities
- Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and its relationship to focal myocardial fibrosis or scar
- Native T1, T2 and extracellular volume (ECV) mapping
- The use and interpretation of T2* imaging in selected conditions
- Imaging patterns reported across hypertrophic, dilated, arrhythmogenic and non-dilated left ventricular cardiomyopathy phenotypes
- How imaging findings are interpreted alongside ECG, family history, clinical assessment and genetic testing
- The value and limitations of imaging in disease characterization and longitudinal assessment
This is an educational research resource. It is not a clinical protocol, diagnostic tool or substitute for specialist assessment.
1. Why tissue characterization matters
Inherited cardiomyopathies arise from genetic changes that affect the structure, function or maintenance of cardiac muscle. The resulting phenotype can vary considerably, even among people with the same disease-associated variant.

Some individuals develop increased ventricular wall thickness. Others show ventricular dilation, impaired contraction, right ventricular abnormalities, or myocardial scar without marked ventricular enlargement or reduced ejection fraction.

This variation makes tissue characterization important. Imaging can identify abnormalities that may not be apparent from chamber dimensions or global systolic function alone.

The 2023 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) cardiomyopathy guidelines emphasize a multiparametric approach. The evaluation combines clinical history, family history, ECG, laboratory findings, genetic information and cardiac imaging. CMR contributes by assessing ventricular morphology and function while also providing information about myocardial tissue.
2. What cardiac MRI contributes
CMR combines detailed anatomical imaging with methods that assess myocardial tissue properties. A typical examination may include several complementary components.
Cine imaging
Cine sequences show the heart in motion. They are used to assess:
- Left and right ventricular volumes
- Ventricular ejection fraction
- Myocardial mass and wall thickness
- Regional and global wall motion
- Ventricular shape and chamber dimensions
These measurements help define the cardiac phenotype and provide a reference for follow-up.
Late gadolinium enhancement
Late gadolinium enhancement identifies areas where gadolinium contrast accumulates differently from surrounding myocardium. In cardiomyopathy assessment, LGE is commonly used to identify focal replacement fibrosis or myocardial scar.

Its location and distribution can provide useful aetiological clues. For example, the ESC guidelines describe patterns reported in different disease contexts, including mid-wall septal enhancement in some LMNA-associated disease and ring-like patterns in some DSP- and FLNC-associated cardiomyopathies.

These patterns are not standalone genetic tests. They must be interpreted with the clinical presentation, family history, ECG and genetic findings.
Native T1 mapping
Native T1 mapping measures myocardial T1 relaxation without requiring contrast. Values can be influenced by several tissue processes, including changes associated with fibrosis, oedema, inflammation or infiltration.

T1 values depend on scanner, field strength, sequence and local reference ranges. They should not be treated as universal thresholds across imaging centres.
T2 mapping
T2 mapping is sensitive to changes in myocardial water content and can help identify oedema or active inflammatory processes in the appropriate clinical setting.

An elevated T2 value is not specific to an inherited cardiomyopathy. It needs to be interpreted in the context of symptoms, biomarkers, other imaging findings and possible acquired causes.
Extracellular volume
Extracellular volume (ECV) is calculated using pre- and post-contrast T1 measurements together with a haematocrit value. It provides an estimate of the proportion of tissue occupied by the extracellular space.

An increased ECV can reflect diffuse interstitial expansion. It is useful in selected cardiomyopathies but is not specific to one genetic cause.
T2* imaging
T2* mapping is particularly relevant when myocardial iron deposition is suspected, such as in cardiac iron overload. It is not a routine marker for every inherited cardiomyopathy, but it can help investigate specific differential diagnoses.
3. Imaging findings across inherited cardiomyopathy phenotypes
The same imaging feature can have different implications depending on the underlying disease. CMR findings should therefore be considered as part of a complete phenotype rather than interpreted in isolation.
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
CMR can clarify the distribution and extent of myocardial hypertrophy, particularly when echocardiographic views are limited or when apical or lateral wall involvement is suspected.

LGE may be present in areas of hypertrophy and at right ventricular insertion points. Its extent and distribution can contribute to clinical assessment, although the absence of LGE does not exclude hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
Dilated cardiomyopathy
CMR can quantify ventricular volumes and systolic function while identifying myocardial scar and other tissue abnormalities.

A mid-wall pattern of LGE is reported in some forms of non-ischaemic dilated cardiomyopathy. The presence and distribution of scar can provide information relevant to aetiological assessment and prognosis, but no single pattern establishes a genetic diagnosis.
Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy
Assessment may include right and left ventricular size, regional wall motion, ventricular function and myocardial tissue abnormalities.

Fatty replacement and scar can be relevant features, but imaging findings need careful interpretation. They should be integrated with electrical findings, clinical history, family history and genetic evidence.
Non-dilated left ventricular cardiomyopathy
A person can have myocardial scar or other tissue abnormalities without substantial ventricular dilation or severe systolic impairment.

CMR is particularly useful in this setting because tissue abnormalities may be an important part of the phenotype even when chamber size and ejection fraction appear relatively preserved.
4. From genetic variation to myocardial phenotype
Inherited cardiomyopathies involve different biological pathways. Depending on the gene and variant, disease mechanisms may affect:
- Sarcomere structure and contraction
- Cytoskeletal integrity
- Nuclear envelope function
- Desmosomal cell-to-cell connections
- Calcium handling and electrical stability
- Mitochondrial function and cellular energy production
- Protein quality control and cellular maintenance
These mechanisms can lead to changes in myocardial structure, cellular injury, inflammation, fibrosis, fatty replacement or impaired contractile function.

CMR does not directly identify the causal genetic variant or reveal every molecular process. Instead, it provides a view of the resulting cardiac phenotype. Genetic testing and family assessment help establish whether the imaging findings fit an inherited disease mechanism.
5. Why ECG and cardiac MRI are complementary
ECG and CMR answer different questions.

An ECG records electrical activity and can identify rhythm disturbances, conduction abnormalities and other electrical features associated with cardiomyopathy.

CMR assesses cardiac anatomy, ventricular function and myocardial tissue. It can reveal structural or tissue abnormalities that are not visible on an ECG.

A person may show electrical changes before substantial structural abnormalities become apparent. In other cases, CMR may identify myocardial changes that an ECG cannot characterize. Using both assessments can therefore provide a more complete clinical picture.

Neither test should be interpreted on its own. Findings are considered alongside symptoms, physical examination, family history, echocardiography, ambulatory rhythm monitoring and genetic results where appropriate.
6. Early disease and family assessment
Inherited cardiomyopathy may develop over time. A relative who carries a disease-associated variant may not initially show the same structural or functional changes as an affected family member.

This is one reason clinical screening can include ECG and cardiac imaging. CMR may provide additional information when echocardiography is inconclusive or when tissue characterization could help clarify the phenotype.

A normal CMR examination does not necessarily mean that a person will never develop features of disease. The significance of a normal study depends on the condition, age, family history, genetic result and clinical context. Follow-up decisions belong to the treating clinical team.
7. Imaging in longitudinal assessment
CMR can also be used to assess changes over time. Serial examinations may help clinicians evaluate:
- Changes in ventricular volumes and ejection fraction
- Development or progression of myocardial scar
- Changes in tissue-mapping measurements
- Evolution of the overall cardiomyopathy phenotype
- Response to selected disease-specific or supportive treatments
Comparisons are most useful when acquisition methods and analysis are consistent. Differences between scanners, sequences and measurement approaches can affect results.

An imaging change should not automatically be interpreted as treatment benefit or disease progression without considering measurement variability and the wider clinical picture.
8. Limitations and interpretation
CMR is a powerful imaging method, but it has limitations.
- LGE detects focal areas of abnormal contrast distribution but may not identify every form of diffuse myocardial disease.
- T1, T2 and ECV measurements are affected by acquisition methods, scanner settings and local reference ranges.
- Imaging patterns can overlap between inherited and acquired conditions.
- Some people with an inherited cardiomyopathy have little or no detectable abnormality on an early scan.
- CMR does not replace genetic testing, ECG, echocardiography or clinical assessment.
- Contrast use and scan suitability require individual consideration by the clinical team.
The most reliable interpretation comes from combining imaging with the patient's clinical and genetic context.
9. Research questions
This repository provides a foundation for examining several connected questions:
1. How do different inherited disease mechanisms produce distinct myocardial tissue changes?
2. Which CMR findings are most useful for distinguishing cardiomyopathy phenotypes?
3. How do LGE distribution and extent relate to disease characteristics and clinical risk?
4. What additional information can native T1, T2 and ECV provide?
5. How can imaging contribute to the assessment of genotype-positive individuals before a clear clinical phenotype develops?
6. How should serial CMR findings be interpreted when monitoring disease progression or treatment response?
7. Where are the limits of imaging when trying to connect a cardiac phenotype to a specific genetic cause?
10. Relationship to inherited cardiovascular gene therapy research
Cardiac imaging is relevant to inherited cardiovascular gene therapy research because it helps describe the disease phenotype before and after an intervention.

CMR measurements may be useful for characterizing ventricular structure, function and myocardial tissue. ECG and rhythm monitoring provide complementary electrical information.

However, imaging findings alone do not establish that a gene therapy is effective. Treatment studies require predefined endpoints, appropriate study design, clinical context and careful interpretation of safety and efficacy data. Changes in an imaging measure should not be presented as proof of clinical benefit unless the study supports that conclusion.

This repository focuses on disease mechanisms and imaging principles. It does not evaluate the efficacy of a specific investigational therapy.

Repository purpose
The aim is to provide a clear, evidence-based reference for understanding cardiac MRI, myocardial tissue changes and imaging findings across inherited cardiomyopathies. The material is organized around cardiac imaging and tissue characterization rather than around a single company, product or therapy.
